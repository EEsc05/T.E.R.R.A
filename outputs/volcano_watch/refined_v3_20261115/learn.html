01 / Measuring the ground

A satellite measures a tiny change in distance.

Radar satellites send a signal toward Earth and record the returning echo. By comparing repeat observations, scientists can estimate how the distance between the satellite and the ground has changed. The method is called interferometric synthetic aperture radar, or InSAR.

What the colours mean

The maps use millimetres of relative line-of-sight displacement. Positive values move toward the satellite and negative values move away. A warm-coloured area is not automatically “dangerous,” and a cool-coloured area is not automatically “safe.”

Why some cells are grey

Water, vegetation, steep terrain and changes between observations can make the radar signal unreliable. Grey cells are masked because the data are missing or fail a quality check. They do not mean no movement occurred.

A map is a measurement with limits.

Atmospheric effects, reference choices and viewing angle can influence a signal. One satellite viewing direction cannot independently resolve full three-dimensional movement.

02 / The forecasting model

Four ways of looking at the same history.

The four-branch hybrid uses dated displacement maps to estimate a future map. It combines complementary forecasts using weights learned from training-data validation folds.

01

Temporal

Follows recent movement over time, with less weight on older observations.

02

Spatial

Looks for recurring patterns across neighbouring parts of the map.

03

Context

Uses movement trends, local variation, observation timing and sensor information.

04

Quality

Uses a smoothed, robust view of the history to limit the influence of unusual values.

Technical detail: temporal EMA, blockwise spatial modes, a support-vector context model and robust smoothing feed a nonnegative weighted combination. This is the only Stage 1 model displayed in this edition.

03 / Knowing what we do not know

Coverage, accuracy and confidence are different.

Data coverage

The share of map cells with usable values. More coverage gives a broader view, but does not guarantee the forecast is correct.

Average error

A summary of differences between saved forecasts and later observations. It describes the evaluated collection, not a guarantee for your chosen volcano or date.

Forecast confidence

The four-branch model has no calibrated probability or prediction interval. This app therefore shows “not calibrated” instead of inventing a percentage.

Deformation is not an eruption probability.

Volcanoes can move without erupting, and some eruptions have subtle or undetected deformation. Seismicity, gas and expert interpretation provide additional context.

Reading a change summary

How much did the deformation map change?

For a saved forecast, the starting map is its actual observed input origin. For a newly generated forecast chain, the starting map remains the original observed map, even when predictions are fed back into later steps. On cells valid in both maps, the app adds the absolute changes and divides by the absolute size of the starting displacement values, then multiplies by 100. It also shows mean absolute change and mean signed change in millimetres.

This is a change in a referenced line-of-sight displacement map. It is not the percentage change in land height, the model’s accuracy, a danger level or a confidence score. If the starting map averages less than 1 mm in absolute displacement, the percentage is withheld because a near-zero denominator can make it misleading.

Forecasts generated from earlier predictions carry a count of predicted inputs and feedback steps. The model’s error can grow as predicted maps replace observations; no numerical confidence penalty has been calibrated for this experimental mode.

04 / Checking a forecast

Compare predictions with observations that came later.

MAE is the average size of a forecast error, in millimetres. RMSE gives more influence to large errors. Lower values are better. Persistence simply keeps the last observed map unchanged; it is the reference used to calculate the improvement shown here.

These are retrospective reporting results on previously used windows. They are not a new untouched test or an operational forecast replay. Some global training observations occur later than reporting forecast origins; results must be interpreted within that limitation.

Sakurajima is training-only in the latest combined Stage 1 run. Its short history does not provide an independent local Stage 1 accuracy score.

Stage 2 is a different question.

The separate seismic experiment asks whether a reported eruption might occur in the next 24 hours. Its current models have not demonstrated useful forecasting skill. A threshold that raises an alarm almost every day does not establish successful eruption prediction.

05 / Sources & responsible use

Follow the evidence back to its source.

Satellite GUNW products are distributed by ASF DAAC, with NISAR and Sentinel-1 histories kept separate. Volcano locations come from the Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program. The experimental Sakurajima activity case uses Japan Meteorological Agency records.
